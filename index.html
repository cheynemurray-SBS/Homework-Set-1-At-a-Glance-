What the class did well

  • Problem definitions were tight. Most of you landed on a single testable question that names the comparison, the outcome, and the population. That’s the hardest part to get right, and it showed.
  • Data quality went beyond a checklist. Nearly everyone addressed the five dimensions: accuracy, completeness, consistency, timeliness, and relevance. The strongest papers also named a specific bias and a specific fix for it.
  • Smart data sources. Several of you chose official records, W-2s, or government data over asking people to remember numbers. That choice alone protects accuracy.
  • Control groups. Both deceptive-advertising plans built a control ad with only the false claim removed. That’s what lets you credit the difference to the claim itself.

Where points were lost

  • No standard of evidence. The highest-scoring papers put a number on what would count as support, like a $50,000 gap at Year 10 or a 10-point difference in belief rates. Words like “substantially” and “significantly” need a threshold behind them.
  • Variables used but never defined. If your analysis uses lost income, MBA cost, or age, it needs a row in your variable table. If it isn’t in the table, you aren’t collecting it.
  • Incomplete variable tables. Every variable needs all seven columns: definition, type, scale, units, why it’s needed, and three to five example values. Remember that type (quantitative, continuous) and scale (nominal, ordinal, interval, ratio) are different things.
  • Sampling that doesn’t guarantee your groups. Stratifying by industry won’t give you an even split of MBA and non-MBA respondents. Stratify on the comparison you actually care about.
  • Arguments with links your data can’t test. If your argument runs A to B to C, check that your data can measure each step. Crash records can tell you about older cars and fatalities, but not how long someone owned their car or what they paid for it.
  • Missing components. All six components are required, each in its own section. An insightful essay that skips the variable table or the display plan will lose those points no matter how good the thinking is.

Before your next submission

Watch your video feedback in Canvas first. It’s specific to your paper. Then run your next plan through this list:

  1. My problem definition is one or two sentences: does X affect Y in Z population?
  2. Every variable my analysis uses appears in my variable table, with all seven columns filled in.
  3. My sampling method guarantees the groups I’m comparing, and my sample size has a reason behind it.
  4. My collection steps are specific enough that someone else could follow them.
  5. I address all five data quality dimensions, plus at least one bias and how I’ll reduce it.
  6. My display plan names one chart and describes both axes.
  7. My analysis plan says, with a number, what result would support my position and what would refute it.

If your score wasn’t what you hoped, come to office hours or catch me after our Tuesday 6 PM session. Most lost points this round were structural, and structure is the easiest thing to fix.
